The post-war decades from the 1950s to the ‘80s changed the Folkestone population as increasing numbers of people moved from the big cities, particularly London. Rebuilding in London was a priority after many houses had been destroyed and become unliveable after the Blitz, but the rate of rebuilding was not happening fast enough for a population rapidly growing as a result of the ‘baby boom’. [^ref1] Increasing living costs saw housing and food accelerate in price, as the government had previously restricted domestic consumption during the war and the increased demand post-war led to inflation. War debt repayments were also prioritised by the government, increasing taxes for Londoners,[^ref2] and a shortage of homes increased housing prices. Deindustrialisation and the closing of the London Docks contributed to an unemployment crisis in a suddenly scarce labour market. Lack of employment prospects, increasing population, mass reconstruction chaos and living cost inflations were primary drivers for Londoners making an escape. At the same time, the government also provided housing in New Towns such as Stevenage, and green belt/rural areas around the country to combat overspill 3. Kent was one of the areas that the government promoted. Many coastal towns in Kent became hotspots for urban native migration. Folkestone specifically gained a significant London-ness during these decades, offering the promise of a calm, scenic and prosperous life which Londoners felt drawn to after a steady decline in urban living conditions.
<br><br>
My own family provide evidence for this shift. My paternal grandfather’s wider family were London natives, moving to Folkestone from Morden near Kingston-Upon-Thames, ultimately contributing to an evolving community and culture in Folkestone alongside many other families moving to the town. Typically, several members of one family travelled together to Folkestone to maintain closeness to their loved ones: something my family also demonstrated as my grandfather’s father, siblings, their spouses and their children all moved together. Migration to Folkestone from urban areas was a family affair rather than an individual one, establishing a new community of Londoners in the town.

The coast has long appealed to people as a holiday location and the manifestation of peace, quiet and relaxation. Folkestone itself once had a particularly high reputation for this; by the late 19th century, the town had established itself as a luxury seaside resort for the wealthy middle-class, boasting luxurious, elegant hotels. The after-shocks of WW2 included irreparable damage to luxury hotels by bombs, and usage by soldiers as coastal bases, lowering the value of a once-prestigious resort.4 The situation was made more difficult by the new-found appeal and accessibility of the holiday abroad. Property prices in Folkestone were affected by the loss of holidaymaker economies, but this in turn attracted working-class Londoners looking to escape the chaos and high cost-of-living of urban life.  
Folkestone offered good transport connections for those who had left people or careers behind in London. Travel to the coast from London had been made more accessible by the Kent Coast Electrification Scheme taking place from 1959 to 1962 5, so returning to London became achievable whenever desired. Some, of course, had no need to return: Folkestone provided a variety of attractions which meant that newcomers had a sense of excitement and an endless supply of fun things to engage with. 
So then, what impact did the arrival of London citizens have on the maritime coastal town? Between the 1951 and 1981 censuses, Folkestone’s population rose steadily and growth occurred across the town’s expanding residential areas.6 Newcomers concentrated around newly developed, rapidly growing working-class housing estates such as Cheriton, the Foord Valley and the Pent Stream corridor, evidenced by the rising housing stock and population density in those areas. The late 1980s particularly saw a growth in population in Folkestone, as Kent drew in workers for the last stages of the Channel Tunnel, attracting individuals and families from wider UK as well as overseas. Londoners had a significant impact.7

During this time, ‘DFL’ (Down from London) was used commonly as a form of insult by Kent locals, who viewed Londoners as loud and largely dismissive of typical Kentish customs, with their fast-paced, urban style of living interrupting the tranquillity of the rural coast. Folkestone itself was reframed as a retirement resort, particularly through the establishment of Saga PLC. At the same time however, the company provided employment for both locals and newcomers.

Folkestone did not just evolve through the arrival of Londoners, but by those leaving for London. Coincidentally, the other side of my paternal family also provide an example for this. My paternal grandmother’s mother was born in Folkestone and subsequently left for London alongside one of her siblings, whilst the rest either stayed in Folkestone or left for Canada. This occurred around the late 1940s or early ‘50s, a time in which people in Folkestone were selectively choosing to move to London with the intention of seeking better employment, social and educational opportunities. 

On a personal note, it is exactly this coincidence that sparked my interest in the effect of Londoners on Folkestone, with both my nan and grandad having connection to London and Folkestone. My grandad and his family were born in London, where he then met my nan, whose mother was born in Folkestone and moved to London before my nan was born. After my grandparents married, my grandad’s family happened to choose to move to Folkestone in the early to mid 70s, if you can keep up with that. 

My grandad, Barry’s, family moved to Folkestone in stages, starting with Barry’s elder sister, Rose, and her husband to 49 Holywell Avenue in the north of Folkestone sometime in the late 1960s or early 70s. Shortly after, Barry’s elder brother, Brian, his wife and their two daughters followed, moving to Clifton Crescent. My great-grandparents were due to join their two eldest children soon after Brian moved but my great-grandmother, also Rose, unfortunately passed away in December of 1973, halting the initial plans of moving. My great-grandfather, Henry, did eventually move to Folkestone sometime in the mid-1970s to the East Cliff area, following through with his and his late wife’s plans where he would live out the rest of his life before he passed away. My grandad was the only one not to follow to Folkestone, marrying my nan, Odette, in Malden where they both opted to move out of London and instead to the aforementioned New Town, Stevenage, where I myself was born and raised.

My nan, Odette, already had plenty of family living in Folkestone. Unlike my grandad Barry, her family originated from Folkestone. My great-grandmother, Margaret, was born there alongside her four other siblings, before she left for London and married my great-grandfather, Richard, born in Lambeth, and had Odette and her sister. Margaret’s father Steven, my great-great-grandfather, owned a FE99 fishing boat on Folkestone harbour, and was presumably a fisherman. Their surname, Goldsack, is known to be concentrated around Kent and Greater London,8 so it’s been a common assumption of my family that we have been set in Kent for many generations. According to my great aunt, we had a poet named Dorothy in the family, though I have not been able to identify her online, and more whimsicly, allegedly the Goldsack family had a pirate ancestor! The only known information about this pirate is the name ‘Captain Goldsack’ and an accompanying poem 9, so one might conclude that he is a work of fiction, but I’ll leave that for the reader to decide; all I know is that it’s always gone down well as a fun family fact! 

For my dad and my aunt, born in Stevenage to Barry and Odette, family holidays to Folkestone were frequent, very convienient for seeing both sides of the family and consequently, rather cheap! They’ve told me many stories of travelling by train from Stevenage into London and across to Folkestone, staying with their aunts and uncles on both their maternal side and paternal side, taking many trips to the beach and harbour, and eating rock sweets and fish and chips. I visited Folkestone for the first time myself when I was around 14, meeting up with my grandad’s side of the family, visiting the house my great-uncle Brian and his wife lived in and seeing the harbour for the first time. 

Earlier this year, now at 22, I visited Folkestone for the second time. My nan Odette sadly passed away a few years ago, and though my nan was not born in Folkestone herself, our family history in Folkestone ties her there. As I visited the harbour again, thinking of her and all of the wonderful stories of my family she had told me, I came across a small crystal jewellery shop, something that my nan would’ve loved. By some strange and wonderful coincidence, the stall actually happened to be named Odette’s Jewellery, and that for me solidified my attachment to Folkestone and the link between the two sides of my dad’s family to Folkestone. To top it all off as a place close to my heart and my heritage, I found out recently that my mum’s paternal grandmother was actually also from Kent! Talk about coincidence. 

As movements changed families, neighbourhoods and the relationship between Folkestone and London, London cockney/Estuary English dialect and traditional Kent dialect merged into something now deemed ‘Estuary Kent’. As my family demonstrates, the movement between London and Folkestone passed through generations, giving Folkestone a special, specific synergy formed through two distinct and cultures.
